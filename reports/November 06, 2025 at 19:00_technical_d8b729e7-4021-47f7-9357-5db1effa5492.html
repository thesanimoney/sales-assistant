Call Snapshot
Technical Context
The call analyzed high false-positive rates in a production clinical trial eligibility matching pipeline for cardiology patients using EHR data. The team investigated error categories after a recent architecture change from GPT-4o-mini extraction to a fine-tuned Llama 3.1 70B extraction model with a rule engine, and discussed short-term mitigations plus medium-term redesign options focused on grounding outputs in source evidence.
Objective
Diagnose why production false positives rose to 23%, contain the most dangerous failure modes quickly, and define an execution plan to evaluate structural fixes for grounding and eligibility extraction.

✓ What Went Well
The team quantified the error modes instead of treating the false positives as a single bucket.
"Three categories. Around 40 of the 95 are what I'd call temporal confusion... About 30 are negation failures... The remaining 25 are what I think are the most concerning — the model is matching on findings that aren't in the record at all."
Why it matters: This is the right engineering move: classify failures by mechanism so mitigations can be targeted rather than retraining blindly.
They identified a probable root cause in the training-label design.
"If your training labels said 'patient is eligible for criterion X' as a positive label, but the labels didn't include the specific evidence in the EHR that supports that judgment, the model learned to predict eligibility without learning to ground it."
Why it matters: This directly explains why a fine-tuned extractor can produce confident but unsupported outputs. It is a strong diagnosis tied to dataset design, not just model choice.
The team prioritized hallucinations correctly based on patient safety and reviewer effort rather than simple count.
"A patient enrolled based on a hallucinated finding is a patient safety issue... Hallucinations require reviewers to verify a negative — searching the entire record to confirm a finding doesn't exist."
Why it matters: This shows good product judgment: prioritize by harm and operational cost, not just frequency.
They challenged initial solution scope when language coverage became a blocker.
"The Swedish records are about 80 percent of our volume. So we can't just solve English and call it done."
Why it matters: This avoided a common trap: shipping a technically elegant solution that does not cover the dominant production dataset.
They defined a fair evaluation plan with a held-out clinically reviewed dataset.
"Same gold set, two systems, compare hallucination rate, recall, precision."
Why it matters: This is the right basis for architecture decisions. It reduces opinion-driven debate and ties the decision to measurable outcomes.
They surfaced data residency before locking the prototype model choice.
"EHR data cannot leave EU jurisdiction. So whatever model we use either has to be hosted in the EU or has to be locally deployable."
Why it matters: This is a critical deployment constraint in healthcare. Catching it in planning prevents wasted prototype work and compliance risk.
✗ What Went Wrong
The current model was fine-tuned and deployed without evidence-grounded labels or held-out clinical review.
"But I'll be honest — we didn't have a held-out clinical review at the time. We trusted the eval loss."
Why it matters: This is a major validation gap. Offline loss metrics were treated as sufficient despite the application being high-risk and evidence-sensitive.
A major architecture swap and criterion-set change were made simultaneously, making regression analysis difficult.
"We didn't measure it cleanly because we changed the criterion set at the same time, so it's confounded."
Why it matters: This is poor experimental control. It delays root-cause isolation and weakens confidence in conclusions about the fine-tuned model change.
The verification pass was initially proposed as if it were a broad fix before being narrowed down.
"That helps with the hallucination category — the 25 cases where evidence doesn't exist — but it won't help with the temporal confusion or the negation failures."
Why it matters: Important correction. Without this challenge, the team could have over-claimed the scope of the short-term fix.
Negation handling complexity was initially underestimated.
"Negation we can probably handle with a dedicated negation detection step using one of the medical NLP libraries — NegSpacy or scispaCy."
Why it matters: That assumption broke as soon as Swedish volume was considered. This is a warning sign that multilingual clinical NLP complexity may still be under-scoped.
The team discussed retrieval latency but did not fully validate end-to-end throughput and infrastructure capacity assumptions.
"The bottleneck would be the retrieval database and the LLM inference endpoint, both of which scale horizontally."
Why it matters: This is directionally true but not yet proven in their environment. Horizontal scaling claims need load testing, especially with healthcare SLAs and private infrastructure.
