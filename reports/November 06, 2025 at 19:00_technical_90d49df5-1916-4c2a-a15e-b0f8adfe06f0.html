Call Snapshot
Technical Context
The call reviewed a production failure in an EHR-based patient eligibility matching system for a cardiology trial. The current architecture uses a fine-tuned Llama model for extracting eligibility signals from clinical records, followed by a rule engine for inclusion/exclusion logic. The team analyzed elevated false positives from clinical review, traced them to temporal confusion, negation errors, and hallucinated findings, and discussed mitigations including verification passes, date-based rule checks, Swedish negation handling, and a retrieval-augmented prototype.
Objective
Diagnose why the production eligibility model is generating excessive false positives and agree on short-term production hardening plus a medium-term architectural path to improve grounding and preserve stakeholder trust.

✓ What Went Well
The team quantified the production issue with concrete failure categories instead of debating symptoms abstractly.
"Three categories. Around 40 of the 95 are what I'd call temporal confusion ... About 30 are negation failures ... The remaining 25 are what I think are the most concerning — the model is matching on findings that aren't in the record at all. Just hallucinating them."
Why it matters: This creates an actionable remediation path. Engineering can map fixes to distinct root causes rather than treating false positives as one monolithic problem.
They identified the likely root cause in the fine-tuning setup: labels captured outcomes but not evidence grounding.
"If your training labels said 'patient is eligible for criterion X' as a positive label, but the labels didn't include the specific evidence in the EHR that supports that judgment, the model learned to predict eligibility without learning to ground it."
Why it matters: This is the core architectural insight of the call. It explains why eval loss looked good while production quality failed clinically.
The team prioritized by patient-safety and reviewer burden, not just raw error counts.
"A patient enrolled based on a hallucinated finding is a patient safety issue."
Why it matters: This is the right engineering-business prioritization. The most dangerous and expensive failure mode was correctly selected for immediate mitigation.
They converted the temporal issue into a deterministic rule-layer fix instead of overusing the LLM.
"Date extraction is already in our pipeline because it's used elsewhere. We just need to apply the criterion-specific recency window when matching."
Why it matters: This reduces model dependence where a programmatic constraint is more reliable, improving system robustness quickly.
They challenged the proposed evaluation for possible contamination before accepting the comparison design.
"If we're testing the retrieval prototype on the same 412 patients that the current model already saw, we have to be careful about contamination."
Why it matters: This is good experimental discipline. It protects the decision from invalid comparisons and overclaiming.
They surfaced compliance early enough to shape prototype scope.
"EHR data cannot leave EU jurisdiction. So whatever model we use either has to be hosted in the EU or has to be locally deployable."
Why it matters: Data residency is a hard architectural constraint. Catching it before implementation avoids rework and sponsor/compliance issues.
✗ What Went Wrong
The model swap went live without a clinically grounded held-out review, despite being safety-relevant.
"We didn't have a held-out clinical review at the time. We trusted the eval loss."
Why it matters: This is a major validation gap. Offline ML metrics were treated as sufficient for a production clinical workflow, which enabled a severe quality regression.
Multiple variables changed at once, making root-cause attribution harder.
"We didn't measure it cleanly because we changed the criterion set at the same time, so it's confounded."
Why it matters: This weakens the evidence around whether the fine-tuned model caused the hallucination increase and complicates future decision-making.
A promising short-term fix was initially proposed without considering the dominant language distribution.
"NegSpacy is English-trained."
Why it matters: The first negation mitigation was not aligned with the actual production environment, where 'Swedish records are about 80 percent of our volume.' This is a reminder that multilingual constraints must be checked immediately.
The team estimated retrieval latency with rough arithmetic but did not validate end-to-end pipeline overhead or failure modes.
"At 100ms each, that's 9,000 seconds of pure retrieval, about 2.5 hours added to your existing 6. So 8.5 total."
Why it matters: This is directionally useful but incomplete. It excludes embedding lookup overhead, model context assembly, queueing, retries, and concurrency bottlenecks that could break the overnight SLA.
They converged on a prototype scope before defining a detailed acceptance framework for production decisions.
"If retrieval brings hallucination from 25 cases out of 412 — 6 percent — down to under 1 percent without sacrificing recall by more than a few points, that's meaningful."
Why it matters: This is a good start but still vague. 'A few points' is not a formal threshold, and no precision floor, review-time target, or confidence calibration metric was defined.
